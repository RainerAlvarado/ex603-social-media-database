# Unit 3 Finding: The spam-removal rate on the reactions dashboard

**To:** Dashboard team
**From:** Trust and Data
**Re:** The "about half of reactions end in spam removal" figure and the creator monetization freeze

The dashboard reported that roughly half of all reactions end up removed as spam, and creator monetization was frozen on that basis. We went back to the underlying records and counted them directly. There are 200 reactions in the data. 156 of them are active and in good standing, 10 are flagged and still under review, and 34 were removed. Of those 34 removals, only 12 were for spam. The others were duplicates, policy violations, or users taking back their own reaction. The real spam-removal rate is 12 out of 200, which is 6 percent, not about half. The established creator who questioned the number was right.

The error came from how the calculation handled blanks. A reaction only gets a removal reason if it is removed, so the 166 reactions that were never removed have that field empty. The calculation meant to set aside one kind of removal and keep everything else, but the database does not treat an empty field as "something else." It treats it as "cannot tell" and quietly leaves the row out, with no error or warning. That shrank the population from 200 reactions to a few dozen, nearly all of them removals, and spam naturally looked like about half of what was left. We could not see the original dashboard calculation, so this is our reconstruction, but it reproduces the reported figure: leaving out user-requested removals this way keeps only 27 reactions, 12 of which are spam, or 44 percent. A second problem turned up in the same review: a step that tried to filter on a calculated value before that value had been calculated. That one fails loudly, so it is less dangerous, but it shows the calculation was never checked end to end.

Our recommendation is to lift the freeze on creator monetization. A 6 percent spam rate does not support withholding payouts across the board. The 10 flagged reactions, which trace back to 7 accounts, are a reasonable thing to finish reviewing, but that is a small, targeted follow-up and not a reason to hold everyone's money. We are handing over a corrected calculation that counts every reaction, has been written three independent ways that return the same accounts row for row, and states plainly how blanks are treated. Before the dashboard goes back into use for decisions, we would ask that the old figure be taken down and that any number driving a payout be checked against a simple total first: if the pieces do not add up to the whole, something has been dropped.
